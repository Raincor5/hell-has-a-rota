# Hell Has a Rota
## World Setting

*Scholar of the First Shift — Prepare to Get Fired Edition*

### The Establishment

Hell has no horizon, but it has a social life.

Behind its blackened towers, condemned residential blocks, endless corridors and windowless departments, people still fall in love. They drink together after their shifts. They celebrate promotions, make enemies over imagined slights, tell jokes about supervisors and pretend not to notice when someone stops coming around.

The Establishment is an immense institution that controls where its inhabitants work, sleep and spend their time. Its buildings sweat in the dark. Its clocks disagree. The oldest records describe it as already understaffed. Nobody remembers arriving voluntarily.

And yet, against all reason, people have built lives here. They have favourite meeting places, bitter ex-partners, private rituals, old friends and reputations they would kill to protect.

The most frightening thing about the Establishment is not that nobody can leave. It is how quickly everyone learns to behave as though this is normal.

### Life Between Shifts

The Rota assigns each soul a place and a duty. It also decides who gets time together, who is separated, and who is suddenly moved to another district without explanation.

Outside assigned hours, the inhabitants gather in crowded common rooms, underground bars, stairwell landings, rooftop shelters and the dim corridors where surveillance has gone blind. There are parties in condemned apartments and whispered conversations in the smoking areas. Music leaks through walls that should have been demolished decades ago.

People flirt, quarrel, reconcile and betray one another. They gossip about who spent the night with whom, who received an undeserved promotion and who has been quietly removed from the Rota. Someone is always celebrating something. Someone else is crying in a bathroom no one admits exists.

The Establishment permits these gatherings because exhausted people are easier to control when they believe they have something to lose.

### The Law of Those Who Were Here First

The Establishment has two hierarchies. One is printed on official documents. The other is older, unwritten, and far more fiercely defended.

In the second hierarchy, authority is measured by how long someone has endured a place. A person who has spent centuries performing the same duty may claim dominion over every newcomer, even if their official rank grants them none. Familiarity becomes ownership. Survival becomes seniority. Seniority becomes a demand for obedience.

A newcomer may have served in the highest halls of the Establishment, survived districts whose names are spoken only in whispers, and mastered work that local veterans have never seen. None of it counts here.

*You were someone elsewhere. Here, you are new.*

The old inhabitants do not necessarily doubt the newcomer's competence. Sometimes they recognise it immediately. That is what makes the arrival dangerous: if skill can earn respect without years of submission, what was all their suffering for?

So they invent trials that measure obedience rather than ability. They correct what is already correct. They issue instructions they have no right to give. They call it tradition, procedure, or simply how things are done here.

The newcomer is expected to respect every title, every friendship, every ancient grievance, every invisible border. In return, the veterans reserve the right to treat the newcomer as though they have no history at all.

Respect flows upward. Recognition does not flow back.

### Reputation Is Everything

Official rank determines what someone can order you to do. Unofficial reputation determines what happens when nobody is watching.

Every district has its circles: veterans who have survived hundreds of shifts, ambitious newcomers seeking protection, charming opportunists, outcasts, and people who seem to know everyone's secrets. Invitations are currencies. Silence is a favour. Being seen with the wrong person can be more dangerous than breaking a written rule.

Rumours move faster than official notices. A careless joke can become a confession by morning. A rejected advance can turn into a disciplinary allegation. A friendship with a supervisor can be mistaken for a promise of advancement—or become one.

Some inhabitants become untouchable because everyone likes them. Others become untouchable because everyone fears what they might reveal.

No one agrees on the truth. Everyone remembers the story.

### Desire, Jealousy and Power

Affection is one of the few things the Rota cannot directly assign. That has never stopped the Establishment from trying to exploit it.

Relationships form across ranks and departments. Some are sincere. Others are bargains disguised as romance, attempts to escape loneliness, or ways to secure a better posting. People promise loyalty in private and deny knowing one another in public. Jealousy turns trivial encounters into vendettas; rejection can have consequences far beyond embarrassment.

Those with authority can offer favours, protection, comfortable assignments and access to places others are forbidden to enter. They can also withdraw those privileges. An unequal relationship may look voluntary from the outside while leaving one person with very little freedom to say no.

Not every romance is corrupt. Not every rumour is true. Genuine tenderness survives here, which makes its destruction all the more painful.

The Establishment rarely needs to force people apart. It only needs to give them reasons to distrust one another.

### Friendship and Loyalty

Some bonds are forged through shared danger. People cover for a friend who cannot finish an assignment, share scarce supplies, warn each other about violent officials, or wait outside a return chamber after a dismissal.

But loyalty has a price. Protecting someone may damage your standing. Telling the truth may expose a friend. Refusing to join a campaign against an unpopular colleague may make you its next target.

Old friendships decay under promotions and resentment. A person who once stood beside you can become your superior overnight. They may insist nothing has changed while expecting you to address them by their new title.

A few refuse to play this game. Their kindness is not naïveté. It is resistance.

### The Social Hierarchy

The Establishment's official chain of command stretches toward a distant authority called **Upper Management**. No one can confirm who occupies its highest offices.

Beneath that structure lies another hierarchy: popularity, attraction, intimidation, favours owed, favours withheld, and secrets shared after midnight. Someone with no title may control a room simply by entering it. Someone with immense formal power may be privately despised by everyone they command.

Supervisors are not all monsters. Some are frightened people who have learned that passing cruelty downward is safer than resisting it. Others enjoy the permission that authority gives them.

A promotion changes more than a person's duties. It changes who laughs at their jokes, who approaches them, who avoids them, and which old acquaintances suddenly insist they were always close.

The Rota governs the shifts. The inhabitants govern each other.

### The Courtesy of Submission

A peculiar etiquette governs the Establishment's social circles: the outsider must know everyone's place, while no one is obliged to acknowledge theirs.

A veteran may interrupt a newcomer in public and call it guidance. A lesser official may demand deference from someone far more capable and call it discipline. A stranger may insist that their years in a single corridor outweigh every hardship survived beyond it.

The most dangerous accusation is not incompetence. It is **arrogance**.

An outsider who speaks with confidence is arrogant. An outsider who refuses an invented order is difficult. An outsider who demonstrates superior knowledge is showing off. An outsider who stays silent is clearly hiding something.

These contradictions are not mistakes. They are a means of keeping the newcomer perpetually on trial.

Yet not everyone accepts the ritual. A handful of inhabitants recognise that authority without responsibility is merely intimidation. They may become the outsider's closest allies—and pay for that loyalty.

### Conversations as Combat

Not every confrontation begins with a weapon.

A supervisor may issue a deliberately impossible assignment. A rival may try to humiliate someone in front of their friends. A colleague may demand an explanation for a rumour. A stranger may offer help at a price they refuse to name.

Knowledge can expose a contradiction. Wisdom can reveal what someone really wants. Charisma can turn an obvious lie into a convincing explanation. Hostility may frighten someone into backing down—or provoke them into attacking. Luck sometimes determines who overhears the exchange.

Winning a conversation does not necessarily mean winning the situation. A successful bluff may shift blame onto an innocent person. Publicly embarrassing an authority figure may earn admiration from one group and permanent hatred from another. Persuading someone to trust you creates a responsibility you may later betray.

There are always witnesses. Even when the room is empty.

### Nights in the Establishment

At night the official corridors grow quiet, but the social districts become restless.

In half-lit gathering places, dismissed employees drink beside the people who reported them. Lovers exchange promises neither believes. Someone boasts about a victory that never happened. Someone else sits alone, watching a familiar face across the room and trying to remember whether they were friends before the last rehire.

Some celebrations continue for days because no one can agree when the night began. Others end when an alarm sounds and half the guests discover their names have appeared on a new assignment.

There are private rooms where favours are negotiated, clubs whose membership lists change after every scandal, and abandoned apartments where people hide from those they once loved.

The laughter is real. So is the fear beneath it.

### Dismissal and Return

Death is recorded as **termination**.

A notice appears: **YOU'RE FIRED.**

When the Establishment needs another body, the terminated employee may wake again in a return chamber. The next notice reads: **YOU HAVE BEEN REHIRED.**

Returning does not restore a life. Your partner may have moved on. Your friends may have divided your belongings. Your enemies may have spent your absence rewriting the story of what happened. Someone who mourned you yesterday may resent your return today.

Repeated dismissal damages memory. Some return unable to recognise their closest friends. Others remember betrayals that everyone else insists never occurred. There are couples who have fallen in love with each other three times and cannot agree which version was genuine.

The Establishment records every return. It records none of the grief.

### The World Beyond the Common Rooms

Beyond the inhabited districts lie abandoned estates, ash-choked roads, sealed institutions and settlements that have attempted to escape the Rota. The Unlisted wander there: souls whose names have been removed from every register. Unlike the protagonist, they once belonged to the Rota and have been deliberately erased.

Some communities beyond the walls have rejected formal authority, only to recreate its jealousies, factions and punishments in miniature. Others maintain fragile networks of mutual aid. The Establishment calls all of them dangerous.

Travel reveals how many different forms a prison can take—and how often its inhabitants build the bars themselves.

### The Unassigned

Every soul in the Establishment is given a designation. Even the dead receive a new posting when their services are required.

The protagonist arrives without one.

Their name appears in the register, but the space reserved for their role is blank. No department claims them. No superior can produce the order that placed them here. The clerks call it an error. The supervisors call it a threat.

They are known, quietly at first, as **the Unassigned**.

There are rumours that the Unassigned served in places far beyond the Establishment's provincial halls—places where competence was tested by calamity rather than custom. Whether these stories are true is left to the player. What matters is the response: the local hierarchy refuses to recognise any standing it did not grant.

The Unassigned is not automatically powerful. They may arrive exhausted, unarmed, and entirely unable to survive a direct confrontation. But they carry something more unsettling than a weapon: a life that cannot be explained by the local order.

Everyone wishes to place them somewhere.

Veterans demand they start at the bottom. Minor officials invent jurisdictions over them. Social circles try to recruit them, claim them, or ruin them. Those who are attracted to their independence may later resent the very freedom that drew them close. A newcomer without an assigned place becomes a blank surface onto which everyone projects their own fears.

The contradiction is absolute: **the only person without an assigned role is the one everyone believes they have the right to command.**

The player may submit, bargain, deceive, fight, form alliances, or rise through the hierarchy. They can learn its rules well enough to exploit them—or discover what happens when someone refuses to play.

But every victory poses a question. If the Unassigned gains authority, will they dismantle the rituals that humiliated them? Or will they finally demand from others the deference they were denied?

### The First Audience

*The newcomer stands before a tribunal of minor officials. Their insignia is tarnished. Their chairs are arranged on a platform barely a hand's breadth above the floor.*

**FIRST OFFICIAL:** "You have no rank here."

**THE UNASSIGNED:** "I had one before."

**SECOND OFFICIAL:** "Then you should know how to show respect."

**THE UNASSIGNED:** "To whom?"

*The chamber falls silent. No one has asked that question in a very long time.*

**FIRST OFFICIAL:** "To those who were here before you."

**THE UNASSIGNED:** "And what do you owe me?"

*The officials exchange glances. Somewhere behind the walls, a bell begins to ring.*

**SECOND OFFICIAL:** "That is not how it works."

*No one explains why.*

### Tone and Themes

*Hell Has a Rota* is a dark-fantasy RPG about social survival inside a decaying institution. It combines psychological horror, interpersonal drama, moral compromise and bitter absurdist comedy.

Its world is populated not by faceless villains, but by flawed people trying to satisfy ordinary needs—love, recognition, security, belonging—under extraordinary pressure. That pressure can turn affection into leverage, loyalty into complicity, and insecurity into cruelty.

The humour is uncomfortable because it is familiar: a catastrophic betrayal dismissed as a misunderstanding, a superior demanding respect they have never earned, a veteran mistaking longevity for merit, a room full of people pretending not to see an obvious abuse of power.

The supernatural horror makes the world strange. Its social life makes it recognisable.

### The Question No One Asks

Everyone wants to know who writes the Rota.

But there is a more troubling question: if Upper Management disappeared tomorrow, would the people of the Establishment finally be free?

Or would they continue assigning shifts, guarding their status, punishing outsiders and tearing each other apart—because after so long, they no longer know how to live any other way?

**The Establishment does not need demons. It has people.**

*And the oldest cruelty is to demand that a stranger know their place while refusing to admit they ever had one.*
